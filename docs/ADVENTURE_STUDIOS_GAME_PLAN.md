# Adventure Studios — Original Game Strategy & Build Plan

Last updated: 2026-11-25  
Status: Game Center concept hub created; game development remains in planning. No playable game, game database, paid service or public game launch has been approved or created.

## 1. Vision

Build an original game studio identity under the Adventure vision. Adventure should create and own its own game concepts and intellectual property—not simply list or resell third-party games.

Games may be:
- Integrated into the Adventure social platform where that genuinely improves the experience.
- Released as independent products outside the social platform, with their own identity and distribution.
- Designed for mobile first where appropriate, with PC/console considered only after the concept, audience, budget and technical requirements justify it.

The studio should make games that are enjoyable on their own and, where relevant, help people communicate, cooperate, make friends and build lasting communities.

## 2. Product principles

1. Originality with evidence: do not claim that an idea has never existed. Research comparable games and products, identify what they do, and define a specific, defensible difference before committing.
2. One excellent first game: do not split effort across many unfinished titles. Choose one flagship, prove the core fun, then expand.
3. Gameplay first: social features must support a genuinely enjoyable game, not hide weak gameplay.
4. Inclusive by design: games for adults, women, men, children and families should welcome their intended audiences without stereotypes.
5. Safe social interaction: consent-based communication, block/report tools, moderation, privacy controls and anti-harassment protections are part of the design—not later add-ons.
6. Child protection: children's games need age-appropriate content, strict privacy, safe defaults, parental controls and carefully limited communication. Do not mix child and adult social spaces.
7. Cost discipline: no paid engine plans, hosting, AI, multiplayer relay, art subscriptions or other recurring services without explicit owner approval and a cost estimate.
8. Protect existing Adventure: game work must not destabilise the social app, its database, authentication, moderation or release readiness. Keep game systems isolated and use separate branches/projects where appropriate.
9. Ownership and rights: create original names, characters, worlds, art, music, code and mechanics. Track asset sources and licences. Do not copy protected characters, branding, assets or distinctive presentation from existing games.

## 3. Candidate game portfolio — ideas, not commitments

### A. Adventure Football — flagship candidate

A football game with a strong multiplayer and club-community layer. Potential roles and features to investigate:
- Create and develop a player identity and avatar.
- Join or build clubs; recruit teammates across countries.
- Play real-time matches and communicate with teammates using safe, controlled tools.
- Progress through competitions, leagues, tournaments and club goals.
- Explore player, manager and club-owner roles if they add meaningful gameplay.
- Keep club life alive between matches through tactics, team planning, friendly challenges and community spaces.

The key design question remains open: should the first version be a more recognisable professional football simulation, or a new football experience with a distinctive gameplay system? Do not settle this until concept workshops and competitor research are complete.

Potential differentiation areas to explore—not promises:
- A genuinely new match control or team-coordination mechanic.
- A new relationship between individual skill, teamwork and club strategy.
- A distinctive progression system that rewards fair play and cooperation.
- A social club life that continues meaningfully outside matches.

### B. Adventure Connections — social and relationship games

Cooperative mysteries, shared missions, conversation games, team challenges and party experiences designed to help people talk and collaborate. Build for consent, age boundaries and safety. Avoid manipulative matchmaking or claims that gameplay guarantees friendship or romance.

### C. Adventure World — family and group experiences

Original cooperative adventures, culture and knowledge challenges, puzzles and creative activities for friends and families. Make the intended age range clear for each title.

### D. Adventure Kids — protected children's experiences

Educational and creative games such as spelling, maths, memory, drawing, animal discovery and age-appropriate adventures. Treat this as a separate safety programme with child-specific privacy and communication restrictions, not an adult social game with cartoon graphics.

### E. Adventure Creative / Lifestyle games

Original creative experiences involving design, fashion, styling, cooking, music, building and storytelling. These should be open to anyone who enjoys them, not marketed through restrictive gender stereotypes.

### F. Adventure Action / Strategy

A possible original action or tactical team game with original worlds, missions, characters and cooperative strategy. This is a later candidate, not a first-build commitment.

## 4. Recommended order of work

### Stage 0 — Protect and prepare
- Keep the existing Adventure social platform and its release gates separate from game development.
- Create a dedicated game strategy document and decision log.
- Do not modify the production social-app database or public navigation for games yet.
- Decide whether the studio identity is an umbrella brand, an in-app label, or both.
- A first static Game Center concept hub now exists at `game-center.html`; it presents the original game portfolio and does not imply that any title is playable.
- Record owner-approved scope, constraints and spending limits.

### Stage 1 — Discovery and originality research
For Adventure Football and any other shortlisted concept:
- List the closest existing games and products across mobile, PC and console.
- Compare their match/game loop, controls, multiplayer, social features, monetisation, audience and weaknesses.
- Identify what players already enjoy and what remains frustrating or underserved.
- Write a clear differentiation statement supported by research.
- Reject ideas whose only difference is a new name, skin or copied feature combination.

Deliverable: a short concept brief with target audience, core fun, unique hook, risks and evidence.

### Stage 2 — Design the first playable experience
- Define the main player fantasy and the repeatable core gameplay loop.
- Sketch the main screens and match/game flow.
- Define controls that work on a phone.
- Decide single-player, asynchronous or real-time multiplayer requirements.
- Define progression, fairness, accessibility and anti-cheat needs.
- Set an intentionally small MVP that proves the core mechanic before adding clubs, chat, tournaments or large worlds.

Deliverable: game design document, screen map, prototype plan and MVP acceptance tests.

### Stage 3 — Feasibility and budget gate
- Compare suitable game engines and networking approaches against the actual design.
- Estimate development time, device requirements, hosting, bandwidth, moderation, support and ongoing costs.
- Identify what can be built and tested on the user's available iPhone workflow and what will eventually require a computer or specialist support.
- Check app-store, privacy, age-rating, copyright, trademark and regional legal requirements.
- Obtain explicit approval before any paid service or purchase.

Deliverable: technical plan, realistic milestone estimate, cost sheet and go/no-go decision.

### Stage 4 — Prototype and prove the fun
- Build only the smallest playable prototype.
- Test controls, performance and the central mechanic before investing in large art or online infrastructure.
- Run structured playtests with appropriate participants and collect honest feedback.
- Fix confusing or boring gameplay before expanding scope.

Deliverable: playable prototype and documented test results.

### Stage 5 — MVP production
Only after the prototype passes:
- Build the first complete game loop.
- Add accounts and multiplayer only where needed, with secure architecture.
- Add moderation, reporting, privacy, anti-cheat and child-safety controls appropriate to the audience.
- Test low-end phones, weak networks, reconnects, latency, abuse cases and accessibility.
- Keep monetisation fair and transparent; no gambling or cash betting.

Deliverable: private test release with clear known limitations.

### Stage 6 — Controlled launch and growth
- Start with a small, invited test group.
- Monitor crashes, performance, retention, moderation incidents and operating costs.
- Fix critical issues before wider release.
- Decide separately whether to integrate the title into Adventure Social, publish it as a standalone game, or do both.
- Expand to additional platforms only when evidence supports it.

## 5. Social connection and safety requirements

For games with communication or relationship features:
- Public/private rooms and clear invitation controls.
- Consent before direct contact; easy block, mute and report.
- Clear community standards and visible moderation routes.
- Anti-spam, anti-scam and harassment controls.
- Do not expose precise location or personal contact details.
- Do not imply that players must share personal information to progress.
- Separate age groups and do not allow adult-child private contact pathways.
- Provide a way to leave a room, team or interaction immediately.

For children's titles, default to no open direct messaging or voice chat. Any communication capability requires a separate child-safety design and parental review.

## 6. Integration with Adventure Social

Do not add a Games tab or game backend to the live social app yet. First decide:
- Whether the game is standalone, integrated, or both.
- What social features truly need to be shared.
- Whether game identity can be linked without exposing unnecessary personal data.
- How blocks, reports and account restrictions interact across products.
- Whether separate game accounts or services are safer than sharing the social platform's database.

If integration is approved later, use a documented API boundary and least-privilege access. Do not let a game client access privileged Adventure tables or secrets.

## 7. First working session when the owner returns

1. Reconfirm the studio vision and whether Adventure Football is the first flagship.
2. Choose the audience and device target for the first game.
3. Compare the realistic-football direction with the radically different-football direction.
4. Build a competitor/research shortlist and identify the strongest gaps.
5. Select one core gameplay hook to prototype—not a full feature list.
6. Agree the smallest testable MVP and what success would look like.
7. Review the technical and cost implications before choosing tools or writing code.
8. Save the approved decisions and next actions in GitHub.

### Decisions still needed from the owner

- Mobile-first only for the first release, or should PC be considered from the beginning?
- Which audience should the first title serve?
- What initial spending limit, if any, is acceptable? Default assumption: no paid services or purchases without explicit approval.

## Owner decisions — Adventure Football direction (2026-11-26)

The owner approved Adventure Football as the first flagship game and selected a realistic professional-football simulation direction, using the broad gameplay quality of contemporary football titles such as EA SPORTS FC 26 as a quality reference—not copying EA branding, protected assets, player likenesses, licensed teams, presentation, or proprietary content.

### Product and release architecture
- First experience: integrate an Adventure Football entry point and game experience into the existing Adventure platform.
- Long-term option: design the game boundary so the football title can later be released as its own standalone product without rebuilding the core game.
- Mobile-first is the working target because Adventure is currently a mobile-first platform.
- Keep game code, assets, services and data isolated from the existing social app. Integration should use a narrow, documented interface; do not expose privileged Adventure database access to the game client.
- Do not change the existing social-app database, authentication, registration status, or release settings as part of early game prototyping.
- No paid engine plans, hosting, multiplayer services, asset packs or other purchases are approved.

### Realism target
Aim toward responsive 3D football with believable player movement and animation, ball physics, passing/shooting, defending, goalkeeper behaviour, formations and tactics, match rules, stadium atmosphere and match presentation. These are quality goals, not claims that the first prototype will contain every feature.

### Delivery sequence
1. Research contemporary football games and define a distinctive Adventure identity and realistic-but-achievable first gameplay loop.
2. Compare game engines and the feasibility of an embedded/in-platform launch with a later standalone build.
3. Define a small playable prototype and test it before committing to full production.
4. Expand toward deeper realism, teams, competitions and social/multiplayer features only after the core game proves fun and technical performance is acceptable.
5. Keep online multiplayer, licensed content and advanced presentation behind separate technical, rights, safety and cost gates.

### Decisions still open
- Initial playable mode and exact first-match scope.
- Target phone performance tier and minimum supported devices.
- Engine and rendering/network architecture.
- Whether the first prototype is offline/single-player before online multiplayer.
- Budget and any paid tools/services (none approved yet).

**Status:** Flagship, realism direction and phased integration strategy approved. Competitor research, game design, engine selection, prototype and cost review have not yet been completed.

## First prototype scope — owner-approved (2026-11-26)

The owner accepted the recommended smaller first playable match before expanding toward full 11-vs-11 football.

### First playable target: 5-vs-5 3D match
- Build a small, complete, playable 5-vs-5 match as the first vertical slice; this is a development milestone, not a promise that the final game will be limited to five-a-side.
- Start offline/single-player against simple AI opponents. Do not build online multiplayer, accounts, clubs, tournaments or live chat into this first prototype.
- Include a compact pitch, two original teams, responsive movement, basic dribbling, passing, shooting, ball/player collision, simple defending and goalkeeper behaviour, goals, match clock and scoreboard.
- Design touchscreen controls from the beginning and profile performance on representative target phones before increasing visual complexity.
- Use placeholder/original low-cost art initially. Do not buy assets or use unlicensed team badges, kits, player likenesses, music or commentary.
- Define clean gameplay modules (match state, input, player movement, ball, AI, camera, UI) so the simulation can later scale toward 11-vs-11 and a standalone release without depending on Adventure social-app internals.

### Provisional engine direction
Unity is the preferred engine candidate for the first prototype because of its mobile workflow, physics/animation ecosystem and broad developer support. This is a provisional recommendation, not a final technical lock: confirm the available development computer, target phone tier, current licensing terms, export/integration path and total prototype costs before creating a production project. Unreal remains an alternative if later visual targets and hardware justify it; Godot remains a lower-cost alternative if the scope changes.

### Prototype acceptance checks
1. A complete match can start, play and finish without a backend or network connection.
2. Touch controls are understandable and responsive on a phone.
3. Passing and shooting produce predictable, testable ball movement and collisions.
4. Opponents and goalkeeper make basic useful decisions rather than standing still.
5. Score, match clock, goals and restart flow work reliably.
6. Performance is measured on named target devices; do not claim mobile readiness without device tests.
7. The game runs as an isolated project and does not require changes to Adventure Social authentication, database, registration or release settings.

**Not approved:** paid software, asset packs, hosting, multiplayer infrastructure, licensed content or purchases of any kind without a separate owner approval.
